Rota basics · 6 min read

What is a work rota?

A work rota is the timetable that shows which shifts you are on and which days you have off. In Britain, that timetable is called a rota. The word comes from the Latin for a wheel, because people take turns. When the hours run overnight, people often call the same timetable a shift rota. When your days off move forward each week, the pattern is a rolling rota. The breaks, and the time off between shifts, come from the Working Time Regulations 1998.

A rota shows the day, the start and finish, and which days are rest days. It might cover one week, or a cycle that repeats. In the United States the same document is usually a work schedule. Some public services call it a roster. In the UK it is a rota either way: a daytime week in a shop, or a run of nights on a ward.

What is a rolling rota?

A rolling rota is a repeating pattern whose days off move, rather than falling on the same weekdays every week. A common example in the UK is 4 shifts on and 4 days off. That cycle is 8 days long, so the next block starts one day later each week. It takes 7 cycles, 56 days, to start on the original weekday again. The guide to what a rolling rota is shows how to count those days off ahead of time. To compare a pattern that moves with staying on the same shifts, see rotating and fixed shift patterns.

Shift rota and work rota

A work rota is the general name. A shift rota is what people call it when the hours are split into changing shifts, often so the workplace can stay open overnight. A shop or a clinic that shuts in the evening is usually talking about a work rota. A hospital ward, an ambulance station or a warehouse is usually talking about a shift rota, with a handover from one crew to the next. The rest rules start in the same place. Nights bring an extra limit, set out with the other rest rules below. The longer comparison is shift rota vs work rota.

The anatomy of a work rota

Every work rota has to balance three priorities: operational cover, contracted staff hours, and legal rest. When you look at your schedule, you will usually see:

  • Shift codes and start times: Common codes include E (Early, such as 07:00 to 15:30), L (Late, such as 14:30 to 22:30), N (Night, such as 21:30 to 07:30), or LD (Long Day, such as 07:00 to 19:30).
  • Rest days: Allocated off-days (usually marked OFF or R). These give you the downtime you need to recover, plan family commitments, and book appointments.
  • Handover windows: Overlaps of 15 to 30 minutes between incoming and outgoing shifts to pass over patient notes, till floats, or site security logs without gaps.
  • The rotation cycle: The pattern of shifts and rest days that repeats. On a fixed rota it returns to the same weekdays. On a rolling rota the cycle is often longer than 7 days, so the days off move forward.

UK legal basics every shift worker should know

Most workers over 18 get the rest below under the Working Time Regulations 1998. A contract, or NHS terms, can give you more. Some jobs follow different rules, including the armed forces, the police and the emergency services. Under-18s have stricter limits.

  • Rest break: One uninterrupted break of at least 20 minutes if your working day is more than 6 hours. It does not have to be paid. That depends on your contract.
  • Daily rest: 11 consecutive hours between working days. Finish at 20:00 and you should not start again until 07:00. Where a job is allowed to cut into that rest, the employer should, wherever possible, give you that time back as compensatory rest.
  • Weekly rest: An uninterrupted 24 hours off each week, or an uninterrupted 48 hours off each fortnight.
  • 48-hour average: Working time should average no more than 48 hours a week, normally over 17 weeks. You can opt out of that average if you are over 18. The opt-out must be voluntary and in writing. Your employer can ask, but you cannot be sacked or treated unfairly for refusing. You can cancel it later, with at least 7 days' notice, or up to 3 months if the agreement says so. Some jobs cannot opt out, including most road transport and airline staff.

If you regularly work at least 3 hours during the night period, you are a night worker. The night period is 23:00 to 06:00 unless you and your employer agree a different period in writing. Any agreed period has to be 7 hours and has to include midnight to 05:00. Night work must average no more than 8 hours in each 24, usually over 17 weeks. You cannot opt out of that limit. Your employer must offer a free health assessment before you become a night worker, and regular assessments after that. You do not have to take the assessment.

Your employer has to give you a written statement of the main terms. For jobs started on or after 6 April 2020, that statement is due on or before the first day, and it must say your normal hours, the days you work, and if those hours or days can change, how that change is worked out. That duty is in section 1 of the Employment Rights Act 1996.

There is not yet a right to a guaranteed number of hours, or to payment when a shift is cancelled or cut short at short notice. Those measures are in the Employment Rights Act 2025, and they do not have a start date yet. The Workers (Predictable Terms and Conditions) Act 2023, which would have let you request a more predictable pattern, was repealed on 6 January 2026. It had not been brought into force.

Sources, checked 10 November 2026: GOV.UK rest breaks, the 48-hour week, night working hours, the written statement, and the GOV.UK timetable for the Employment Rights Act 2025 (updated 5 November 2026). The regulations themselves are the Working Time Regulations 1998 and section 1 of the Employment Rights Act 1996. The repeal of the 2023 predictable-terms Act is section 7 of the Employment Rights Act 2025, in force on 6 January 2026.

Quick tip for reading your rota

Always check overnight shifts carefully: a night shift starting at 20:00 on Friday spans into Saturday morning. Confirm if your workplace counts the shift on the start date or splits the hours across the midnight boundary for pay and holiday purposes.

Why tracking your own rota matters

The published rota only tells you what was planned in advance. Real-world shifts often end late, swaps happen at short notice, and extra hours get added during busy weeks. If you only rely on a printed rota pinned to the staff room noticeboard or an awkward hospital portal login, it is easy to lose track of what you actually worked versus what you were scheduled to do.

Keeping your own copy on your iPhone lets you check upcoming shift rotations at a glance, calculate your estimated take-home pay before payday, and spot any payslip errors calmly without relying on scrap paper notes.

Frequently asked questions about work rotas

Plain answers on what a work rota is, what rota means, rolling rotas, shift rotas, and UK rota law.

What is a work rota?

A work rota is the timetable that shows which shifts you work and which days you have off. It names the start and finish of each shift and marks the rest days. You will see one in shops, hospitals, care, hospitality and the emergency services.

What does rota mean?

Rota means a schedule of turns. In the UK it is the usual word for the timetable of who is working and who is off. The word comes from the Latin for a wheel, because people take turns. In the United States the same document is usually a schedule, and some public services call it a roster.

What is a rolling rota?

A rolling rota is a repeating shift pattern that moves onto different weekdays, rather than keeping the same days off each week. On a 4-on, 4-off pattern the cycle lasts 8 days, so the next block of shifts starts one day later each week. It takes 7 cycles (56 days) to start on the original weekday again. How to count the days off is covered in what a rolling rota is. The difference between a pattern that moves and staying on the same shifts is covered in rotating and fixed shift patterns.

What are the UK laws on work rotas?

For most workers over 18, the Working Time Regulations 1998 give a work rota these limits: an uninterrupted break of at least 20 minutes on a working day over six hours, 11 consecutive hours off between shifts, an uninterrupted 24 hours off each week or an uninterrupted 48 hours off each fortnight, and an average week of no more than 48 hours. The 48-hour average is normally measured over 17 weeks, and you can opt out of it in writing if you are over 18. The opt-out has to be your choice. You cannot be sacked or treated unfairly for refusing, and you can cancel it later with at least 7 days' notice, or up to 3 months if the agreement says so. The 20-minute break does not have to be paid unless your contract says so. Some jobs, including the armed forces, the police and the emergency services, follow different rules, and some road transport and airline jobs cannot use the opt-out. Where a job is allowed to cut into daily rest, the employer should, wherever possible, give that time back as compensatory rest. Your employer also has to give you a written statement of the main terms. For jobs started on or after 6 April 2020, that statement is due on or before the first day, and it must say your normal hours, the days you work, and if those hours or days can change, how that change is worked out. That duty is in the Employment Rights Act 1996. There is not yet a right to guaranteed hours, or to payment when a shift is cancelled at short notice. Those parts of the Employment Rights Act 2025 do not have a start date yet. The Workers (Predictable Terms and Conditions) Act 2023 was repealed on 6 January 2026 and had not been brought into force. If you regularly work at least 3 hours between 23:00 and 06:00, you are a night worker, unless a different 7-hour night period including midnight to 05:00 is agreed in writing. Night work must average no more than 8 hours in each 24. You cannot opt out of that limit. Your employer must offer a free health assessment before you start night work, and at regular intervals. You do not have to take it.

What is the difference between a shift rota and a work rota?

A work rota is the general name for the timetable of shifts and days off, and a shift rota is that timetable when people work changing shifts, often through the night. A shop or clinic that closes in the evening will usually call its timetable a work rota. A hospital, an ambulance station or a warehouse will usually call it a shift rota, because one crew hands over to the next. Both start from the same rest rules. Regular night work adds the 8-hour average, which you cannot opt out of. A fuller comparison is in shift rota vs work rota.

What do shift codes on a work rota mean?

Shift codes are shorthand abbreviations used on a work rota grid to indicate shift types, start times, and rest days at a glance. Standard UK shift codes include E (Early, often 07:00 to 15:30), L (Late, often 14:30 to 22:30), N (Night, often 21:30 to 07:30), LD (Long Day, commonly 12.5 hours on hospital wards), and OFF or R (Rest day). Workplaces also use administrative codes such as AL for annual leave, S or SK for sickness, and TR for mandatory training.

Why should you track your own work rota separately?

You should track your own work rota separately because published schedules only record what was originally planned, not the actual hours you worked on shift. Handovers run late, short-notice swaps happen verbally, and emergency overtime often goes unlogged on communal noticeboards or trust portals. Keeping a private record on your phone helps you spot payslip mistakes, verify unpaid break deductions, and plan family time around rolling rest days.
