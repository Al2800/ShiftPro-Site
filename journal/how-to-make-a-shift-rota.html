How to make a shift rota

Here is how to make a shift rota that holds up in practice: calculate your total weekly coverage hours, apply a realistic relief factor (usually 1.20 to 1.25) to cover leave and sickness, choose shift lengths with handover buffers, and schedule forward-rotating cycles that protect legal rest. Building a multi-shift or 24/7 rota requires an accurate staffing formula so your team never runs dangerously short or burnt out.

Most shift rota problems start with simple arithmetic errors. A planner counts the hours needed on shift, divides by 37.5 contracted hours, and hires exactly that headcount. The schedule works for two weeks until someone takes annual leave or calls in sick, and the entire team gets pulled into emergency overtime. Designing a dependable shift rota means planning for real-world absence from day one. (If your team runs standard daytime opening hours rather than continuous handover cycles, compare shift rota vs work rota differences first to choose the right scheduling model).

1. Calculate your weekly coverage hours

Start by calculating the total hours of presence your operation requires across seven days. Do not look at staff names yet; look strictly at the posts that must be manned:

  • 24/7 continuous post: 24 hours × 7 days = 168 hours per week per position.
  • Two-shift service (e.g. 07:00 to 23:00 daily): 16 hours × 7 days = 112 hours per week.
  • Acute care post with handover overlap: Two 12.5-hour shifts daily = 25 hours × 7 days = 175 hours per week.

If you run a ward that requires three nurses on day shifts and two on night shifts, you multiply your daily hours accordingly. Every extra post multiplies your total requirement.

2. Apply a realistic relief factor

In the UK, full-time employees are legally entitled to 5.6 weeks of statutory annual leave (28 days for someone on a five-day week). Add average sickness absence (typically 3% to 4% in public services) and mandatory training days, and the average worker is unavailable for roughly 20% to 25% of their contracted hours.

To stop rotas from collapsing under normal staff leave, calculate your relief factor:

  • A standard UK relief factor is between 1.20 and 1.25.
  • Take your single 24/7 continuous post (168 hours). Divide by 37.5 contracted hours = 4.48 full-time staff needed just to cover the baseline.
  • Multiply by a 1.22 relief factor: 4.48 × 1.22 = 5.47 full-time staff per post.

This tells you straight away that a 4-person team cannot run a 24/7 post without persistent overtime or external bank staff. You need a 5-team or 6-team rotation pattern to absorb holidays safely.

3. Choose shift lengths and handover windows

Choose 8-hour, 10-hour, or 12-hour shifts depending on what fits your service best:

  • 8-hour shifts: Traditional early, late, and night schedule. Easier on physical endurance, but requires three handovers every 24 hours and gives staff fewer full rest days off each month.
  • 12-hour shifts: Usually 07:00–19:30 and 19:00–07:30. Long days are demanding, but staff compress full-time hours into three or four shifts per week, leaving longer stretches of uninterrupted rest. For healthcare rotas, read our guide to NHS shift rota patterns to see how ward long days and nights, handover overlaps, and unsocial hours pay are set up.
  • Handover overlap: Always specify a 15-to-30 minute overlap in the official shift hours. If your outgoing shift finishes at 19:00 and the incoming shift arrives at 19:00, handovers inevitably push staff past their finish time, leading to unrecorded overtime and tired handovers.

4. Build forward-rotating cycles

When assembling shifts into a repeating sequence, follow the physiological principle of forward rotation: Earlies → Lates → Nights → Rest days. Your body adjusts much better when your sleep schedule moves forward rather than backward.

Check every sequence against the UK Working Time Regulations 1998 (for standard shift codes and statutory rules, see our guide on what is a work rota):

  • 11 consecutive hours of daily rest between the end of one shift and the start of the next. Avoid the "clopening" trap where an employee finishes a late shift at 22:30 and opens at 07:00 the next morning (only 8.5 hours off).
  • 24 hours uninterrupted rest every 7 days, or 48 hours every 14 days.
  • Night worker protections: Average night shifts cannot exceed 8 hours in a 24-hour period over a 17-week reference window unless covered by specific collective exemptions.
The rota builder's sanity check

Before publishing a master shift schedule, trace one person's line through every week of the cycle. Check that rest blocks after nights are at least 48 hours long and that weekend shifts are shared evenly across all lines. Follow our checklist in how to make a fair staff rota to ensure unsocial hours stay balanced.

5. Test standard patterns before creating your own

You rarely need to invent a schedule from scratch. Standard industry patterns have been refined over decades to solve coverage and rest balance:

  • 4-on, 4-off: 2 days, 2 nights, 4 days off. Simple 8-day cycle, excellent 4-day recovery blocks.
  • 2-2-3 (Pitman): 14-day cycle that guarantees every alternate weekend off.
  • Continental: Fast-rotating 28-day schedule with frequent short breaks.

Read our breakdown of shift rota patterns and templates to see how each cycle works in detail. If your team has simpler, single-shift coverage requirements, check our guide on how to make a work rota for small teams.

Common questions

Frequently asked questions about making a shift rota

Direct answers on coverage hours, relief factors, shift lengths, UK rest rules, 24/7 patterns, and how shift rotas differ from daytime rotas.

How do you calculate coverage hours for a shift rota?

To calculate coverage hours for a shift rota, multiply the number of staffed posts required by the operating hours per day and the days per week. For example, a single post that must be staffed around the clock requires 168 hours of coverage each week (24 hours multiplied by 7 days). If a care home or control room needs two staff on duty during the day and one at night, calculate the daytime and night posts separately and add the totals together. This raw figure gives you the exact baseline hours your team must deliver before accounting for contracted worker hours or annual leave.

What is a relief factor and why is it needed on a shift rota?

A relief factor is a multiplier applied to your baseline staffing numbers to ensure you have enough extra staff to cover planned holidays, sickness absence, and training. In the UK, full-time workers receive 5.6 weeks of statutory annual leave (28 days for five-day staff), while public sector sickness averages between 3% and 4%. A typical UK relief factor sits between 1.20 and 1.25. Without factoring this in, a continuous 24/7 post that technically needs 4.48 full-time staff on 37.5-hour contracts will collapse into emergency overtime or costly agency cover whenever someone takes holiday or phones in sick.

What is the best shift length for a 24/7 shift rota?

The best shift length depends on your service demands, with 8-hour, 10-hour, and 12-hour shifts each offering clear trade-offs between physical stamina and recovery days off. Traditional 8-hour shifts (earlies, lates, nights) reduce single-shift exhaustion, but they require three handovers every day and leave staff with only two rest days per week. In contrast, 12-hour shifts compress a 37.5-hour contract into three or four shifts per week, creating longer stretches of uninterrupted rest, which is why acute hospital wards favour them. Whichever shift length you choose, schedule an explicit 15-to-30 minute handover overlap so outgoing staff do not stay late unrecorded. You can explore clinical shift timings in our review of NHS shift rota patterns.

What legal rest rules apply when making a shift rota in the UK?

Under the UK Working Time Regulations 1998, a shift rota must provide at least 11 consecutive hours of daily rest between shifts, 24 uninterrupted hours off every seven days (or 48 hours every fortnight), and a 20-minute rest break for shifts exceeding six hours. Rotas must also ensure night workers do not average more than 8 hours of work per 24 hours over a 17-week reference period. Shift schedules must avoid “clopening” combinations, where an employee finishes a late shift at 22:30 and returns for an early opening shift at 07:00 without the 11-hour recovery window. If an urgent operational emergency interrupts this rest, employers must grant equivalent compensatory rest immediately afterwards. Read our statutory summary in what is a work rota.

How many staff do you need to cover a 24/7 shift pattern?

You need a minimum of five full-time staff members to cover a single around-the-clock post safely without relying on permanent overtime. One continuous 24/7 post requires 168 hours of coverage each week. Dividing 168 hours by a standard 37.5-hour contracted week yields 4.48 staff, and applying a realistic UK relief factor of 1.22 raises the total headcount to 5.47 full-time equivalents. Established multi-crew rotations like 4-on 4-off, Continental, or 2-2-3 (Pitman) distribute these hours across four to six crews to balance weekends and maintain legal rest. See full cycle breakdowns in our guide to shift rota templates and patterns.

What is the difference between a shift rota and a work rota?

A shift rota coordinates continuous, multi-crew handovers across 24/7 or extended hours, whereas a standard work rota schedules staff across daytime opening hours. Shift rotas rely on repeating mathematical cycles (such as 4-on 4-off or rotating earlies, lates, and nights), handover overlaps, night worker limits, and unsocial hours pay enhancements. Daytime work rotas typically adjust week by week to customer footfall and end when the building closes.
